How to write better prompts for AI image generation

Most prompting advice tells you to add more words. That advice is wrong, and understanding why will fix more of your images than any list of magic phrases.

← All guides The app

Describe the picture, do not give an order

You have probably spent a while now telling an assistant what to do. Say "make it brighter" and it makes it brighter. An image model does not work that way, and it fails quietly: you still get a picture, it just isn't the one you asked for.

There is no "before" for it to act on. Your words do not instruct the picture — they become it. So "change to a man" paints the words change, to and man, and two of those three mean nothing at all. Nothing is broken. The model drew what you typed.

Write the finished picture instead. Not what you want done to it, but what you want to see.

change to a man                      an order   — almost nothing happens
a man with short dark hair           a picture  — this one works

The same rule holds anywhere the app takes words. "Fix the hand" describes a problem; "a hand gripping a knife" describes a picture. Only one of those is a thing that can be drawn.

There is a hard limit, and you will hit it sooner than you think

The model stops reading at about 60 words. Everything after that is thrown away — not treated as less important, thrown away. If your carefully considered lighting note sits at the end of a long prompt, the model never saw it at all.

The exact figure is 77 of what the model calls tokens, which are roughly words but not exactly: common words cost one each, unusual ones cost two or three. So "a red fox at night" is five, while a long compound word might be four on its own. Sixty words is the number to keep in your head.

This produces a failure that feels like the model ignoring you, when really you were cut off mid-sentence.

Order matters more than length

Words near the front carry more weight than words near the back. So the fix for a prompt that is not working is usually not to add a clarifier at the end — it is to move the thing you care about to the front.

If the style is what matters, lead with the style. If the subject is what matters, lead with the subject. What you write first is what you get.

Short prompts often beat long ones — when you start from nothing

This surprises people. Faces are where models are most fragile, and a long prompt makes them worse — it crowds out the model's own knowledge of what a face looks like with your adjectives. Ask for a portrait in eight words and you will usually get a better face than in forty.

Use the length you have for things the model cannot guess: the setting, the light, the framing. Do not spend it describing what a person's eyes normally look like.

All of that holds while the picture starts from nothing. Restyling a photograph is the opposite case, and the reason is worth having: your words are no longer the only thing in the room. Every pixel of the photograph is also saying what the picture is.

Ask a blank canvas for "a man" and one word is enough, because nothing disagrees with it. Ask a photograph of a woman for "a man" and that one word is outvoted — by long hair, by a jaw, by shoulders, by a dress. It is not that the model ignored you. It is that you were outnumbered.

So when you restyle, do both: say what the subject is, and name the visible things that have to change. Neither half works alone, and it is worth seeing why.

a man                            nothing moves. One word against a whole photograph.
short dark hair                  the hair changes, and only the hair.
a man with short dark hair       half way. The face shifts, the body does not.
a man with short dark hair,      this one lands.
  bare shoulders, seen from
  behind, looking back

Read that as a dial rather than four attempts. A restyle does not switch the picture from one thing to another, it blends — and how far it travels tracks how much of the picture your words account for. Name one feature and one feature moves. Name the subject, the pose and the framing, and the whole thing comes with you.

Everything you leave unsaid is supplied by the photograph, and it will happily hand you a half-finished change: the new hair on the old shoulders. That is not the model misunderstanding you. It is the model doing exactly the proportion of the work you described.

Do not fight the style you chose

If you pick an illustration style and then write "photorealistic, sharp focus, DSLR", you do not get a compromise — you get a hybrid that looks like neither. The model resolves conflicting instructions by averaging them, and averages of two styles look wrong in a way neither style does.

Pick the style, then describe the content in neutral language and let the style do its job.

A structure that works

Subject first, then setting, then light, then framing. Stop there.

a red fox sitting on a snowy park bench, under a streetlight, at night

subject ──────── setting ──────────── light ────────── time

That is fourteen words and it specifies everything that matters. Adding "highly detailed, 8k, masterpiece, award winning" to the end changes almost nothing except pushing something useful past the limit.

Change one thing at a time

When an image is nearly right, resist rewriting the whole prompt. Change a single word and generate again.

This matters more than it sounds, because a word does not adjust the picture — it rewrites the scene. Here is the same prompt four times, with the same seed, changing only the last two words:

a red fox sitting on a park bench under a streetlight, at night
a red fox sitting on a park bench under a streetlight, at dawn
a red fox sitting on a park bench under a streetlight, in fog
a red fox sitting on a park bench under a streetlight, in heavy snow

"At night" gives a tight portrait with the fox facing the camera. "At dawn" pulls the camera right back to a receding street with autumn colour in the trees. "In fog" goes wider still and drops the fox small into the frame. "In heavy snow" adds a second fox that nobody asked for.

Nothing else changed. Not the subject, not the bench, not the random seed. Two words moved the framing, the distance, the palette and the number of animals in the picture.

That is why you change one thing and look. Rewrite five words at once and you cannot tell which one did what — and the next prompt you write will be a guess. Change one, and after a dozen images you are writing prompts that work first time, which no article can teach you directly.

Negative prompts are for exclusion, not quality

A negative prompt steers the model away from things. It is useful for "no text", "not blurry". It is not a quality dial — stacking twenty negative terms does not produce a better picture, and past the same token ceiling the later ones are discarded exactly like positive ones.
