How to upscale a photo to 4K on your iPhone

Enlarging a photo and upscaling a photo are different operations, and the difference is why one looks soft and the other does not.

← All guides The app

Resizing versus upscaling

When you resize an image, the software spreads the pixels it already has over a larger area and guesses the gaps by averaging neighbours. Nothing is added. A blurry photo becomes a larger blurry photo.

An upscaling model has been trained on millions of pairs of small and large versions of the same picture. It has learned what fabric weave, hair, brickwork and foliage look like close up, so it reconstructs plausible detail rather than averaging. That is why the result looks sharp rather than smooth.

What this can and cannot do

It is very good at texture: skin, fur, fabric, stone, leaves. It is good at edges that were soft because the image was small rather than because the shot was out of focus.

It cannot recover information that was never captured. A photo taken out of focus stays out of focus — sharper, but still wrong. A face too small to be more than a few pixels will not become a recognisable person; the model will invent a plausible one. And upscaling a heavily compressed image will faithfully sharpen the compression artefacts along with everything else.

Doing it on the phone, offline

Upscaling runs comfortably on modern Apple hardware, and unlike generation it does not need a huge model — the one we use is about 33 MB and ships inside the app, so it works from the moment you install and never touches the network.

Practically: pick the picture, choose how much larger you want it, and let it run. Output is capped at 4096 pixels on the longest side, which is more than enough for a large print or a desktop wallpaper. On an M-series iPad, a full 4096-pixel result takes roughly ten to twelve seconds.

Why size is a choice, not a maximum

Bigger is not always better. If you want a cleaner version of a picture at the size it already is, ask for 1× — you get the detail reconstruction without the file becoming enormous. Doubling is usually the sweet spot for something you will look at on a screen. Reach for the maximum when you are printing.

Adjusting afterwards

A sharper picture often wants small corrections — a little more warmth, the shadows lifted, the highlights pulled back. Doing that after upscaling rather than before means the adjustments apply to the detail that was reconstructed, not to the softer original.
